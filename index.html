Looks can't tell you.

A breeder pairs two birds and waits a whole season for eggs that never come. A collector pays for a rare isopod and receives a common one. The eye is a poor judge of what DNA settles in days.

From feather to finished report.

The same six steps sit behind every result, and the last one hands you the evidence.

  1. Request a kit

    Tell us what you need tested. We send a free collection kit with instructions and return postage, or you drop off in New Jersey by appointment.

  2. Collect and send

    Pluck a few feathers, fill a blood card, swab, filter water or pack a dead specimen. Each sample gets its own ID the day it arrives.

  3. We extract the DNA

    In a clean pre-PCR area we break open the cells and purify the DNA. Tubes, pipettes and gloves never travel back from the post-PCR area.

  4. PCR copies the target

    A thermocycler copies only the gene that answers your question, about a billion times in 30 cycles.

  5. We read the result

    Bird sexing is read on an agarose gel. Species and microbe IDs are Sanger sequenced and matched against reference databases. eDNA is read by species-specific PCR.

  6. Your report, with the evidence

    A signed report with a QR code that opens the raw gel photo or sequence trace. We keep your extracted DNA for 90 days, so re-tests are free.

What's in every run.

A run only counts if its controls pass. If one fails, we repeat the run. We never report it.

  • Known male and known female birds. They prove the test can tell the sexes apart in this run.
  • Extraction blank. A tube that goes through extraction with no sample. If DNA turns up, something was contaminated.
  • No-template control. PCR with water in place of DNA. Any band here and the run is repeated.
  • Outside checks. Each quarter, a few blind duplicates go to another lab to check our answers.

What we don't accept

  • Human samples of any kind. We are not a clinical lab.
  • Feathers of native North American wild birds. Captive-bred cage birds and poultry only.
  • Live invertebrates. Send dead, dried or frozen specimens.
  • Any sample sent to diagnose disease.
